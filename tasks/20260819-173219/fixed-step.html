The fixed step, and why the frame rate is not a rendering problem

The average frame was never the defect. The worst one was, and it is made of simulation. This is what nine measurement lanes found, what got fixed, what got ruled out with a number, and the one decision still waiting.

2026-08-21 dev profile, headless arena duel unless stated every figure measured unless marked ESTIMATE

01 The shape of the problem

The game runs two clocks. The frame clock draws as fast as the machine can. The fixed clock runs at a constant 64 Hz, so physics and fixed-clock logic advance in slices of 15.625 ms of simulated time, whatever the frame rate. That is what keeps a collision at 30 fps identical to one at 144 fps.

Each real frame, the engine runs however many fixed steps it owes. And the cost of one step is what this document is about.

02 Why a step at 58% of budget is not "fine"

If a frame takes longer than 15.625 ms of wall time, the engine owes two steps next frame. Two steps cost twice as much, which overruns harder, which owes more steps. It is self-amplifying.

Every other hitch in the game gets multiplied by the same factor. So the step cost is not one line item among many. It is the coefficient on all of them.

at 5 ms1.5×32% of budget
at 8 ms2.0×51% of budget
measured 9.022.4×58% of budget
at 12 ms4.3×77% of budget
at 15.6 ms∞never catches up

Observed in the wild: up to eight fixed steps in a single frame, and frames of 112 ms.

03 Where the step actually goes

The assumption going in was that this is collision cost, and that four thousand colliders at six solver substeps explains it. Measured off avian's own phase timers, on a heavy 1v1 fight slice, that assumption is wrong.

The observation that reframes everything

Contact constraints never exceeded 51, and average two to four. Almost nothing in a fight ever actually touches. The three to four thousand "contacts" are AABB overlap pairs produced by speculative margins on fast-moving bullets: they cost broad and narrow phase time and almost never reach the solver.

So six substeps re-integrate roughly 900 bodies, six times over, to resolve two contact manifolds. The cost is not collision. The cost is having 900 bodies - and about 770 of them are rounds in flight, even on a quiet step.

04 Everything tried, and its verdict

Run the fixed loop single-threaded kept

The fixed schedules are small and run 64 times a second, and the multithreaded executor charges a task fan-out per schedule per run that they cannot amortise.

Step median 7.89 -> 6.08 ms (p=.032). Frame p99 36.9 -> 21.0 ms. 1% low 27.1 -> 47.5 fps (p=.001). Avian's own physics schedules were deliberately left multithreaded: single-threading those measured nothing on the step and made the frame tail worse (p99 36.9 -> 40.6, p=.038), because the solver's parallel passes are the one part that genuinely saturates threads.

Fuze torpedoes off the target's own colliders kept

The old code did a world-wide point projection and declined foreign colliders in a predicate. A declined proxy returns infinity, which never tightens the traversal's search radius, so the walk degraded toward the whole tree - per torpedo, per frame.

1% low 23.3 -> 30.0 fps. Worst frame 57.2 -> 41.0 ms.

Scope the scenario collision observers kept

Two global observers meant every collision in the world dispatched into the scenario crate and was declined on the first query.

22,241 wasted invocations -> 0, in a range containing no areas and no crates. Point-defence p99 8.4 -> 6.9 ms.

Reduce solver substeps ruled out

6 -> 4 moves nothing (p=0.92). 6 -> 2 is net worse: median step 8.33 -> 10.55 ms, because peak contact constraints explode 55 -> 1,085 during rams and hulls visibly interpenetrate. 6 -> 1 degrades point defence (1% low 122 -> 62 fps) and changes outcomes. Six is correct.

Stop sections of one hull testing each other ruled out

Structurally absent. One rigid body per hull, and avian skips same-body pairs outright. There was never a fix to make.

Sleep idle or distant hulls ruled out

40-41 bodies already sleep, and the point-defence controller's torque wakes every ship every tick anyway.

Batch the collision-event consumers ruled out

Bought nothing (step median p=.49, 1% low p=.61) - the estimate rested on 12,673 events, but that was a whole-run total and a fight step raises 100-180. It also moved a saturation invariant (peak live rounds 2,420 -> 2,190, p=0.001), because the game leans on the impact and bullet consumers interleaving per contact.

Archetype fragmentation, asset loads, log volume, pipeline compilation ruled out

Archetypes 527 -> 525, flat, with 20 archetypes holding 89% of entities. Mid-run asset loads peak at 0.07 ms and never on the main thread. Suppressing 52,446 log lines made p99 worse. There is no render sub-app headless at all.

A round is not a physics body owner's call

Swept projectiles: no per-round body, collider or tree churn. Damage inputs preserved exactly. This is the item section 03 points at.

05 A rejection that was wrong, and why it was wrong

Earlier in this epic, "a round should not be a physics body" was closed rather than escalated. The evidence was a single number, and the number was correct: collect_collision_pairs<ProjectileHooks> measured 0.192 ms of a 26 ms frame.

The cost of a round being a body was never in pair collection

It is in the per-step bookkeeping every body pays: integration, AABB update, tree update, writeback, transform propagation. That is spread across a dozen systems, none of which has "projectile" in its name.

So we profiled the one system named after the suspect, found it cheap, and closed the case. The general lesson, which cost this epic several days: a cost spread evenly across many systems is invisible to a ranked profile, and naming one system after the suspect makes it look exonerated.

06 What the instruments got wrong

Five confidently wrong conclusions were produced during this work, all by careful people following the protocol. Each came from the measuring apparatus, not the game.

  • A measurement flag that changes the subject. Arming the capture makes the arena field a 4v4 instead of a duel. A whole comparison of "1v1 against 4v4" was 4v4 against 4v4.
  • Windows indexed by frame count. Three instruments held their window for a number of frames. Headless runs five to ten times faster, so each became a fifth of the simulated duration it was sized for - which made a correct point-defence fix look like a regression, and made the census report an identical scene for two different rosters.
  • A trace that stops before the phase you are asking about. The default traced run never reaches the fight, so a per-system table described loading and approach. Visibility work topped it, because that is what dominates a cheap frame.
  • A gate that opens onto the aftermath. The arena's capture waited for both teams to have dealt damage - and a team wipe credits damage, so the gate could open on the frame the fight ended. Now refused explicitly.
  • Step counts read backwards. A slow frame owes steps, so a high step count on a slow frame can be an effect. The tell that it is genuinely a cause here is that per-step cost rises with frame time, which a pure catch-up artefact would not do.

All five are now either fixed or written into the conventions. The habit that caught each of them was the same: compare like with like, and prefer a count over a millisecond.

The fixed step is now under the 8 ms ceiling on a duel. Rounds-as-bodies is the one remaining lever large enough to reach the 5 ms target, and it is a gameplay decision rather than a presentation one, which is why it is not being taken quietly.

The mechanic it touches stays either way: collision damage is how bullets do damage, and the swept-projectile form preserves the damage inputs exactly. What changes is whether a round spends every substep being integrated, AABB'd, tree-updated and written back in order to resolve, on a typical step, two contacts.

Nova Protocol / task 20260825-221015 / editor polish, step 21

What earns a place on the stage

The editor's chrome is docked panels on a CRT. Three candidates wanted to be things in the room instead. One of them already was, one of them earns it, and the rest are panels for a reason. No window system was built.

Spike Shipped: ship nameplates Dropped: floating readout, gallery card

01 The question

Every readout in the node editor lives in a rail or a panel: the scene tree and the engineer block on the left, the inspector on the right, the menu bar and the breadcrumb across the top, the key legend along the bottom. It is a console, and a console is a good answer for most of it.

But some readouts are about one thing in the room, and reading them means looking away from that thing, finding the panel, and then looking back. The step asked which of those earn an anchor on the stage, before any floating-window machinery gets written to give them one.

02 The rule

A surface earns a stage anchor when all three hold

  1. One thing. It answers a question about a single object in the room, not about the document, the tool, or where the editor is.
  2. Eyes on it. The reader has to be looking at that object while they read - the answer is useless without the thing in view.
  3. Read, not operated. Nothing on it is typed into, dragged, or scrolled. A field that moves with the camera is a field you cannot finish typing into.

Failing any one of the three means docked chrome. The three tests are cheap to apply and they settled every surface in the editor on the first pass, including the two the step named as maybes.

03 The three candidates the step named

already anchored

A placement refusal beside the ghost

Built before this pass. The placement callout follows the ghost and says why the part will not go there. It passes all three: one socket, eyes on the ghost, nothing to operate. It is the precedent the plates copy - a screen-space node placed from world_to_viewport every frame, no interaction, no chrome.

stays docked

A floating readout that belongs to its ship

Fails test 2. Turn, mass, thrust and part count are read while placing a part, to watch a number move - the eyes are on the socket, not on the hull as a whole. Fails test 1 too: inside a ship there is exactly one ship, so "which one is this about" was never a question. It reads better in the rail, where it holds still and does not cross the thing it describes.

stays docked

A gallery card that opens where the part is

Fails test 1 and test 3. The gallery is a list being browsed: the reader is choosing between parts that are not in the room yet, and the card is scrolled and clicked. An anchor would need a thing to anchor to, and at browse time there is none.

05 The one screen: hull nameplates

The rail names every node and the stage named none. At the scenario node the default world holds a built ship, five derelict hulks and three pickets, and the only way to find out which grey shape is Derelict Hulk 3 was to click each in turn and read the rail. That is the failure the rule was written to catch.

What is plated, and when

Hulls always. The edited ship and the seeded spacecraft alike - there is a handful of them, they are the document's citizens, and telling them apart is the whole problem.

Everything else only while marked or under the pointer. A range of fifteen rocks wearing fifteen labels is a rail, not a stage. A rock takes a plate for exactly as long as its name is the answer to a question being asked, then gives it back.

Scenario node only, like the grid. Inside a ship the stage holds one hull and the breadcrumb already names it.

Plates hang in their own layer under the placement callout: a verdict about the part in hand outranks a label on a rock behind it. Each one is placed from its node's own collider bounds - over the top of the hull, never its middle, because a label in the middle of a hull is a label on the hull. A node that is behind the eye hides rather than pinning itself to a corner, and the set is rebuilt only when it changes, in the rail's own id order so two runs of one document put the same plates in the same place.

06 Why no window system

Both anchored surfaces the editor now has - the callout and the plates - are one-way, non-interactive, and about sixty lines each. Neither needs dragging, focus, z-order between siblings, occlusion against geometry, or a title bar. A window system would have bought all of that machinery for two labels that never asked for it.

The grading table is the reason to expect that to hold: every surface that failed the rule failed it on test 1 or test 3, and test 3 is precisely the test a window system exists to serve. Nothing in the editor is both about one thing in the room and operated.

Revisit when a third anchored surface appears that has to be operated - a per-node handle that is dragged rather than read, say. Until then the pattern is the layer, not the window: a screen-space node, positioned from the node's own bounds every frame, Pickable::IGNORE, and no chrome.
